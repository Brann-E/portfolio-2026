The detail

The problem as posed

A steering tool that became an obstacle

Nexans, a global player in cables, steers its sales business in Switzerland from an eighteen-page Excel file. The system is slow, illegible, and no longer absorbs changing needs. Sometimes you need a calculator to find a figure that the table should give you.

Loading times reach thirty minutes. Daily backups and updates become a chore in themselves. On several occasions, entire business units find themselves at a standstill, unable to open the file. After repeated escalations, management decides to migrate to a Power BI solution.

The old tool, blurred for confidentiality: instructions written in yellow in the cells, to know how to read it.

Turn the Excel into a modern tool. Reduce loading times. That is what I was asked to look at.

Put that way, it's a technical migration problem: moving existing data to a better interface, with better response times.

The shift

We could display everything, and decide nothing

I had full access to the users - business unit directors, sales managers, field salespeople - for qualitative and quantitative research. What comes out of the interviews is more troubling than an ergonomics problem.

Users struggle to state their need. Not through incompetence: because the tool trained them to look at figures, never to ask questions. The system's complexity created a disconnect. Many simply avoid it, and those who use it look for information without knowing what they'll do with it.

On the product side, the rigidity of the architecture makes every change request costly. On the business side, adding a view or integrating a dataset is such an investment that the business unit gives up. The system is frozen and expensive, and each iteration freezes it further.

But the common pattern, at every level, lies elsewhere. The tool started from the available data and then looked for what it could be used for, never the reverse. The decisions the organization had to make, and what it took to make them, had never been set as a starting point.

It wasn't a decision tool. It was a data display, in the hope that decisions would follow.

The shift comes down to a pivot: moving from a data-driven approach to a business-centered, data-informed strategy. We no longer start from what we can measure, but from what the business needs to settle, and data goes back to being a means instead of the subject.

The instrument

Atomic Metrics, or how to get people to say what they want to know

The practical problem was this: if users can't express their data need, you can neither question them head-on nor decide for them.

Hence the Atomic Metrics method, which I designed and ran in workshops. Each metric gets an identity card that asks four questions: what it is for, how it is built, what it connects to, how it could be visualized. In workshops, participants complete these cards together, and in doing so they make visible their priorities, the relationships between indicators, and the questions that really matter to them.

The result is not a list of KPIs. It's a shared base from which the BI pages, its navigation and its data logic take shape. A card says not only what we want to measure, but where that measurement should appear and how we will interact with it.

We don't ask people what data they want. We build with them what they want to know.

The open field

A metric on its own always ends up lying

Designing from decisions rather than from data brings up a problem the old approach hid: a good metric doesn't just measure, it steers. And as soon as it steers, it becomes a target and therefore degrades.

This is Goodhart's law, or the cobra effect: when a measure becomes a goal, it ceases to be a good measure. It's not an accident, it's a property. As soon as an indicator becomes the single focal point, teams find ways to optimize it, often at the expense of quality, ethics or the long term.

The answer I built isn't to find the perfect metric, but never to let a single one decide.

The engine

The primary metric, the one that drives the organization's performance. It provides power and makes action measurable.

The compass

The guard metric, the one that keeps the course on the business's real goal and stops people chasing misleading signals.

An engine alone moves without direction; a compass alone points without moving. What produces accuracy is neither one nor the other, but the tension maintained between the two. This pair structures the metric cards, and through them, the whole system.

On the home page, the pair reads effortlessly: real revenue against budget drives, real sales margin keeps the course. Selling more is pointless if the margin collapses. Same logic elsewhere: orders booked keep an eye on the share that is running late.

The sheet made available to the teams frames each pair: the goal, the engine, the compass, the tensions, the risks, where it appears and who answers for it. Here it is, filled in for this one.

What it produced

A tool teams make their own, and that grows with them

The redesign resulted in an information architecture that follows decisions rather than data tables: five core pages shared by everyone, and three pages adapted to the specific needs of each business unit. A readable information hierarchy, powerful filtering, and the end of cluttered tables in favor of an immediate overview.

What I keep from it

What this project anchored in my practice

On the tool

The tool is never the end goal. It is the vessel of decision;
and data that questions nothing is just one more passenger.

I had been asked to display figures better. The work consisted in understanding what this organization was trying to know, and discovering that it had never asked itself, because the tool answered before the question was posed. A dashboard is not worth what it shows, but the decisions it makes possible.

On the necessity of information

Nothing deserves to exist by default. Any data, any information that illuminates no decision must be questioned.

Since this project, I ask the same question of every piece of information I put somewhere: what does it let us settle that couldn't be settled without it? The one that can't answer isn't neutral: it takes up space, diverts attention, and makes people believe we know something. I now apply this demand to every screen, every feature, every step of a journey.

What fed this perspective

The Tyranny of MetricsJerry Z. Muller

This reflex of starting from the decision rather than from the data has a source I gladly own: The Tyranny of Metrics, by Jerry Z. Muller. It is the book that gave me the words for what I saw at work at Nexans.

Muller starts from an observation summed up by Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a goal, it ceases to be a good measure. We end up optimizing the figure, no longer what it was supposed to say. The cobra effect is the most telling illustration: by rewarding every cobra killed to reduce their number, people were pushed to breed them.

The eighteen-page Excel was exactly that: indicators so numerous and so closely tracked that they had ended up replacing the decision they were meant to illuminate. I kept a reflex from it: never ask which figures to display, but which decision they must serve.
