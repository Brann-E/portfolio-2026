The bond matters more than the good. The gift, or the oldest machine for making things circulate.

Where does the impulse to give come from? There is a mechanism, as old as human societies, that makes things circulate without a contract and forms the strongest alliances. It comes down to a single thing: a debt that is never settled.

12 min read

The previous texts showed how a circulation becomes visible and actionable. They didn't say where it comes from; what, concretely, pushes someone to give their time, their attention, their knowledge to another, with no guarantee of return.

This question seems naive. It is in fact the deepest of the whole series, because the answer contradicts everything our era takes to be self-evident about exchange.

In 1925, the anthropologist Marcel Mauss published in L'Année sociologique a text that would become one of the most commented in the social sciences: the Essay on the Gift. In it he studies societies - the tribes of the American Northwest, the Melanesian islands - where the social bond is woven first by the gift, and not by market exchange. This is a point Polanyi would establish in his own way: the self-regulating market is only a late historical form of economic organization, long embedded in the social rather than towering over it. There, the gift is not a soul added to an economy; it takes its place. We give, we receive, we give back. And this cycle is not kindness, it is the very backbone of the social bond, what holds groups together.

What the gift is not

We must set aside the misunderstanding right away. The gift Mauss speaks of has nothing to do with charity, with the free and disinterested gesture we spontaneously imagine. It is a system of obligations: in these societies, one must give, one must receive, one must give back. To refuse to give, to refuse to receive, to refuse to give back is to declare war - it is to cut oneself off from the bond.

These systems have names, and they are neither marginal nor vanished. The potlatch of the peoples of the Northwest Coast of America, where one establishes rank by giving - sometimes by destroying - more than one's rivals. The kula of the Trobriand Islands, that vast maritime circuit where shell necklaces and armbands, with no use or market value, circulate from island to island forming lifelong alliances. The moka of the Melpa of Papua, where one gains importance not by what one owns but by what one distributes, by placing the other in debt. The dāna of the Indian world, a ritual gift that runs through Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism. And the Morgengabe of Germanic law, that nuptial gift the husband publicly hands over the day after the wedding, of a value that far exceeds what custom would require.

Five areas, several continents, several millennia. These are not five interchangeable versions of one system, nor the relics of an age of the gift to which we should return. They are five situated ways of making the gift an operator of relation, alliance, debt, prestige or repair. Bringing them together is not meant to confuse them: it is meant to recall, against modern utilitarian self-evidence, that the social bond could - and still can - be tied otherwise than by market equivalence. Mauss, moreover, doesn't content himself with the Oceanian or American societies he calls, in the vocabulary of his time, “archaic”; he looks for the same logic in our own legal roots - the Germanic wadium, that pledge which is not a material guarantee but an object carrying a part of the one who commits it, or the nexum of ancient Roman law, where the thing handed over durably binds persons. The gift that obliges is not an outdated stage of exchange, surpassed by the market: it is a foundation that runs through cultures and law, our own included.

Mauss calls this the system of total prestations: it is not individuals exchanging objects, it is entire groups obliging each other, through things that are never mere commodities. For the given object is not inert. Mauss takes up the Maori term hau - the spiritual power of the gifted thing, a notion whose interpretation remains debated - to say that the gift carries with it something of the giver, and that it is this power, attached to the thing, that compels the return. What circulates in the gift is not the thing: it is a part of the person.

In the gift, it is never the good that matters. It is the bond it opens, maintains or repairs.

This is the decisive reversal, and it is at the heart of all this work. We think we give things. We give relationships. The object is only the medium, the vehicle, the pretext of a circulation whose real stake is the bond itself. To offer is not to transfer a good: it is to make, or revive, a relationship. That is why a gift repaid in money is an insult - the vehicle has been mistaken for the message.

Positive mutual indebtedness

The engine remains to be understood. Why does the give-receive-return cycle never stop? Why isn't it settled once and for all, like an invoice?

Because - and this is the finest point - the counter-gift never equals the gift. It always differs: by timing (we don't give back immediately, that would be a repayment), by nature (we don't give back the same thing), by value (we give back a little more, or a little differently). This inequality is not a flaw in the system: it is its very spring. As long as the debt is not exactly settled, the relationship stays open, alive, in motion. Each remains, toward the other, in a state of light debt - and that debt is what makes you want to meet again.

We still have to agree on what goes into such a debt, because it has almost nothing to do with what the word usually evokes. Here, money is almost never in question. We are in debt because we received a story, advice that mattered, a joke that eased a difficult moment, an attention that landed just right, knowledge passed on without ceremony, time we didn't owe. Most of what circulates this way passes through language - speaking to each other is already going into debt to one another, and it is very exactly by this that you enter into a relationship with someone. These debts, impossible to quantify and that we don't even think of settling, are what weave the bond. The day we could repay them all at once, there would be no relationship anymore - only accounts up to date.

A debt you settle closes the relationship. A debt you maintain keeps it alive. The whole secret of the gift is there.

This is what we can call positive mutual indebtedness: a regime where everyone feels indebted to the other, not for a precise sum to repay, but for a bond to honor. Far from being a burden, this reciprocal debt is the fuel of circulation. It is the exact opposite of the market transaction, which aims for nobody to owe anything to anybody anymore - that is, for the relationship to be able to end with nothing left over.

Here we have the anthropological foundation of the whole series. What makes things circulate durably between people is neither the contract, nor pure altruism, nor well-calculated interest: it is this open debt, never closed, which makes it so that after receiving we want to give in turn. Circulation is not maintained by balance. It is maintained by imbalance - an imbalance we never seek to resorb entirely.

The third paradigm: leaving the individual and the system

Starting from Mauss, the sociologist Alain Caillé and the MAUSS movement - the Anti-utilitarian Movement in the Social Sciences - drew a major theoretical consequence, which directly illuminates the genealogy of the first text.

The social sciences, they say, oscillate between two great rival paradigms. Individualism explains everything by the interest of calculating individuals - this is utilitarianism, the logic of the market, homo economicus. Holism explains everything by structures, norms, constraints imposed on individuals. On one side rational atoms, on the other a structure that determines them. Yet neither accounts for the gift - this act that is neither a self-interested calculation nor pure obedience to a rule.

The gift therefore opens a third paradigm: a third way of understanding the social bond, irreducible to the other two. Neither the isolated individual nor the overarching structure: the relationship as primary reality, as that from which one must think. This is exactly the shift the first text asked of design - leaving the isolated object (the individual) and the abstract system (holism) to take circulation between beings as the starting unit.

Structuralism and form. Caillé levels at structuralism a reproach that also applies to systemic design: by dint of explaining phenomena through underlying structures, it forgets to account for what really happens in the exchange - the living form a relationship takes, the event of the gift, what plays out between people and that no structure predicts. It is the same blind spot: seeing the structure and missing the circulation.

Conditional unconditionality

One last concept, and it is Caillé's subtlest, because it settles a formidable objection. If the gift rests on an obligation to return, isn't it at bottom a disguised exchange, an interest that dares not speak its name?

The answer lies in an openly embraced oxymoron: conditional unconditionality. The authentic gift contains a part of the unconditional - we give first, with no guarantee, as a bet of trust open to the other. It is this initial, uncalculated gesture that opens the relationship and sets it absolutely apart from the contract, where nothing is done before the conditions are laid down. But this unconditional only survives over time if certain conditions are honored - if the bet is, at least sometimes, repaid. We bet without guarantee; but a bet always lost ends up fading.

We give first, unconditionally - that is the bet of trust. But this bet only holds if, over time, it is honored now and then.

This is not a contradiction, it is the very structure of every living bond. A friendship starts with a free gesture and is maintained by a reciprocity that is never counted but never quite absent. A commons is founded on an unconditional openness and protects itself with a few conditional rules, without which it would be plundered. Designing relational arrangements is precisely holding both: making room for the unconditional part that opens - the bet, the welcome, the first gesture - without which there is no relationship; and setting the conditional part that protects - the minimal rules, the limits. This conditional part is not there only to make things last: it is a measure of preservation. It protects the bond from abuse, and it protects the one who gives from burning out or being stripped bare. It is the old wisdom of well-ordered charity that begins with oneself: giving unconditionally does not mean sacrificing oneself without limit. An unconditional that protects itself nowhere doesn't last - it gets plundered, then it fades.

The symbol, or what must be drawn

One consequence remains, and it is addressed directly to the designer. For this circulation to take place, it must be recognizable, the protagonists must know that a gift is a gift, that an opening is an opening. This is the role of the symbol.

The symbol is what makes circulation tangible and shareable: a gesture, a form, a rite that signifies “this is a gift”, “this commits us”, “this opens something between us”. But a symbol only works if its meaning is shared - if it is the object of a prior agreement, tacit or explicit. A symbol the other doesn't decode makes nothing circulate; both sides must agree on what it means. That is why there are rites where meaning is produced: the rite is the performative form that institutes recognition - it doesn't describe the bond, it makes it exist by the very fact of performing it.

And this is very precisely the work of relational design: to find, to draw the symbols - the forms, the objects, the rituals - through which a circulation becomes recognizable and therefore possible.

Where the anthropologist reads the meanings already at work, the designer proposes new forms for meanings still without a symbol.

The gift makes what invents circulate

One might think this mechanism is reserved for archaic societies or the intimate sphere. That is false, and two contemporary inquiries into innovation confirm it, each in its own way - closing what the first text advanced about Verganti and Epstein.

David Epstein shows that discoveries are born from the circulation of knowledge between distant fields, not from specialization closed in on itself - what passes from one field to another is exactly what, in the gift, passes from one person to another: what belonged to no one becomes fertile by circulating. Safi Bahcall, a physicist, shows for his part that an organization's ability to bring forth breakthrough ideas depends not on its culture but on its structure - on its ability to let fragile ideas circulate rather than freeze them. He makes it a matter of phase transition: a hair's breadth of structure separates the organization where ideas circulate from the one where they freeze, as a degree separates water from ice.

Making things circulate is not a softness added to efficiency. It is the condition of everything new that comes about - alliances, discoveries, worlds.

The gift, innovation, discovery say the same thing in three forms: what is fertile is never the thing closed in on itself, it is the passage, the alliance, the setting into circulation. We now have the complete foundation: circulation holds the world, it is seen in a shared threshold, it becomes action through common knowledge, and it is maintained by the gift and its open debt.

It remains to see what happens when all this stops, and what we can learn from it. When nothing circulates anymore. It is there - in the ordeal of the negative - that the thesis of this series reveals its true stake, which is not only one of design, but of a far more serious order. That is the subject of the last text.
