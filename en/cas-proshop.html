The product

I was asked to disintermediate sales. The sales relationship was precisely what held the company together.

Alaska Energies, now MyLight150, distributes supplies for positive-energy buildings. Its e-commerce platform, the Proshop, accounts for only 2.5% of sales despite repeated investment: illegible journey, abandoned carts, faulty catalog.

I come in as product designer to rebuild it, with rare access: the entire sales chain, from buyers to warehouse staff, and the customers themselves.

The detail

The problem as posed

A platform that costs a lot and sells nothing

Alaska Energies distributes supplies for positive-energy buildings. Its Proshop, the online ordering platform, piles up difficulties: an incomprehensible order journey, transaction errors, filters that don't work, obsolete stock showing out-of-stock at payment time.

The result is damning: despite substantial investment and a redesign attempt already aborted, the platform accounts for only 2.5% of sales. Carts are abandoned, customers don't come back, and support teams absorb in tickets what the tool can't do.

Rebuild the Proshop. Simplify the buying journey.
That is what I was asked to look at.

The urgency is real: all over Europe, competitors offer journeys where you order in three clicks. Put that way, it's a classic e-commerce problem: a funnel to smooth, a catalog to clean up, an interface to redo.

The answer was already written into the order: disintermediate sales, before even looking at who sold to whom. It is the trap of the premature solution, an answer settled before the question is posed.

The shift

What was taken for a brake was the main asset

I had rare access: the whole sales chain available for research - purchasing, field salespeople, sales administration, in-store salespeople, logistics, finance, marketing, support - and customers matching the target profile (ICP: Ideal Customer Profile). Forty interviews, internal and external.

Examples of ICPs, to identify the customers to prioritize in our research.

The expected diagnosis was confirmed without surprise: 40% of carts abandoned because of an overly complex order funnel, inaccurate stock, incomplete product descriptions, teams losing their days repairing what the tool doesn't do. Redundancies everywhere, looping processes, frustration settled on both sides.

But it was by listening to customers that the perspective shifted. What they described was not a wish to do without salespeople. It was the opposite! The relationship with the sales teams was what they valued most about this company: product knowledge, advice on a job, troubleshooting. What they wanted was to be able to act alone where the salesperson added nothing: a restocking order, an already known item, an end-of-day emergency.

They weren't asking for less relationship.
They were asking to choose when it serves them most.

The brief assumed that a good platform disintermediates sales, that progress consists in removing the human from the journey. The research said the opposite: in this market, the human was the competitive advantage. The problem wasn't that there were salespeople in the loop; it was that nothing equipped them, and that the customer had no autonomy when they wanted it.

The value proposition, with two entries: a single offer had to answer the customer and the internal teams at the same time. It is this cross reading that showed the human was not the friction to remove, but the asset to equip.

What was circulating in the sales chain. The thick line, trust and advice between the customer and their salesperson, is what disintermediation was about to cut; the dotted pink line, the load that support absorbed in tickets.

What was circulating

  • Trust and advice, between each customer and their salesperson: this is what disintermediation was about to cut.
  • Product knowledge and troubleshooting, which only went through the sales teams.
  • A load, that of support, which absorbed in tickets what the tool couldn't do.

The instrument

A cross-channel model, where the tool becomes the support of the relationship

From this shift follows the model that structured the whole project: a single platform where all orders are placed, but carried by different actors: the customer themselves, the field salesperson, the in-store salesperson.

The reversal is simple to state and changes everything. We no longer build a sales channel that competes with salespeople; we build the common tool they all rely on. The customer gains autonomy without losing their contact. The salesperson stops keying in purchase orders and goes back to advising. And the company gets a single view of what sells, whatever the path taken.

The tool doesn't replace the relationship.
It becomes its support.

The open field

Once sales is thought through cross-channel, everything can be articulated

Once the model is set, a set of workstreams opens up, none of which could be formulated as long as we thought of the e-commerce funnel as isolated.

01

Explicit commercial rules

Simplified discounts, clarified shipping terms and rate scale, defined credit-limit rules, framed year-end bonuses, and a gross margin calculation mechanism that validates or blocks a sale before it goes out.

02

An interconnected platform

The Proshop stops being an island: connection to the CRM, ERP, WMS, TMS and CPQ, so that data flows instead of being re-entered at every step.

03

Loop-free processes

A key function assigned to an identified person to break the back-and-forth, and a lead salesperson assigned to each customer to handle their problems end to end.

04

B2B finally addressable

Bulk orders, an entry journey for major partnerships, and an architecture designed to absorb growth rather than endure it.

These four workstreams go beyond e-commerce. As soon as the tool serves the relationship, you are designing a sales model, and no longer just a purchase funnel.

What remains

The reasoning was right. The alignment didn't follow.

The work produced a set of deliverables that laid the foundations of the new sales model:

  • 01Benchmark of the solutions able to host the Proshop
  • 02Taskflows of the new sales model for each department, and blueprints where the tech stack was committed
  • 03Impact matrix to prioritize features
  • 04Information architecture and wireframes of the product pages
  • 05Sitemap covering all product families and their filters
  • 06Audits of solutions and vendors against the stated ambitions

The sales model played out on a real journey: a customer receives a field salesperson for a new need. Each actor and each system has its own lane, from first contact to delivery.

The product information architecture, distilled: a single attribute reference serves navigation (filters with two entry points) and the structure of each product page (four levels, from category to detail).

The blockage did not come from the model, but from what it asked people to admit. Management saw in digitalization a way to reduce the sales force, and took for granted that sales would follow on their own. The cross-channel model said the opposite: value didn't come from the channel, it came from what happened between salespeople and their customers; and a tool that cuts that link doesn't produce savings, it produces a loss.

The model asked management to change a belief: the sales relationship as a cost. Donella Meadows ranks this level, the paradigm, among the most powerful leverage points of a system, and among those that resist the most. The halt of the project illustrates it.

This is a consequence that's hard to picture until you've seen the whole system. The deliberation on the technical solution dragged on, the scope slid toward a services portal - informing the customer, leaving the ordering to the sales teams as before - then the project stopped.

My practice?

What this project anchored in my practice

On the product

A tool doesn't replace a relationship;
at best, it gives it the means to exist.

I had been asked to remove the human from the sales journey, because that is what good e-commerce does. The research showed that in this company, the human was the advantage, and that customers weren't asking for less relationship, but for the right to choose when it serves them. This project is the first where I formulated what became my central question, four years before I knew how to name it.

On decision-making

Sound reasoning isn't enough to bring a project to completion;
everyone who decides must be able to see the same future in it.

Those who decided weren't wrong to want savings; they simply couldn't see what the model made possible, because they were looking from a place where the sales relationship is a cost and not an asset. Since then, I no longer treat alignment as an end-of-project step, but as material to work on from day one, alongside the solution.

Where I leave the object paradigm

The Design of Everyday ThingsDon Norman

This one, I cite for what it taught me as much as for the precise point where I part from it: The Design of Everyday Things, by Don Norman, the book that trained generations of designers - myself included - to make objects obvious to use.

Norman is right about the object: a good tool needs no manual, it guides the hand without a word. But a whole discipline drew a dogma from it: the best design is the one that perfects the object, until it is so autonomous that it removes the intermediary and makes the human superfluous. This is the object paradigm, and the Proshop brief stemmed entirely from it: disintermediate sales through a product, erase the salesperson from the journey.

This case is exactly where I stopped designing objects to design relationships. The object paradigm doesn't see what this field was crying out: the human wasn't the friction to remove, they were the asset to preserve. I kept Norman's demand for obviousness, but I moved it from the object toward the bond: designing a tool not so that it replaces the relationship, but so that it gives it the means to exist. All my work since starts from this shift.

The sequel - and the step he didn't take

Emotional DesignDon Norman

It is Norman himself who revisits these positions. Twenty years after teaching a whole profession to make objects simply usable, he writes Emotional Design to acknowledge what the first book left aside: we don't love an object because it works, and a perfectly functional object can leave us cold, even become hateful to us.

He describes three levels at which an object reaches us: the visceral, the immediate emotion of the senses; the behavioral, the pleasure of use; and the reflective, the deepest, what the object says about us, the memories and meaning we attach to it. In other words: the value of a thing doesn't lie in the thing, but in what happens between it and the person living with it. The master of the functional object admitted, in his way, that function is never enough.

But Norman stops at the threshold this case made me cross. His emotion remains that of a person facing an object: the teapot you cherish, the car you love to drive. At Proshop, affect didn't sit between the customer and the platform - it sat between the customer and their salesperson. What Norman opens toward the emotion of the object, this field forced me to open one notch further: toward the emotion of the bond, between people. Where he designs an object you love, I design the tool that protects the relationship you don't want to lose.
