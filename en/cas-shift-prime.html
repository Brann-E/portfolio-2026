The product

I was asked to fix a platform. Everyone was right from where they stood, and that is exactly what was blocking.

SHIFT Prime is the platform Nexans uses to steer its marketing campaigns worldwide, from local teams up to the executive committee. It is built on Microsoft Project Accelerator, a project portfolio management solution built on the Power Platform (Power Apps, Power Automate, Power BI).

It is in place on paper, but adoption isn't taking off. I lead the discovery to understand why, and to frame what needs to change.

What if the biggest disagreement was the one nobody had ever been able to have?

The detail

The problem as posed

A platform in place, and adoption that doesn't come

Nexans is deploying the SHIFT Prime platform to steer its marketing campaigns worldwide, built on Microsoft Project Accelerator, designed to give visibility on initiatives, budgets and performance, from local teams up to the executive committee.

The finding is clear: teams aren't taking it up. Steering remains blurry where the platform was supposed to illuminate it, and the levels of the organization don't agree on what they are looking at.

Redesign the platform. Improve the experience.
That is what I was asked to look at.

Put that way, it's a product problem: an interface to rework, features to adjust, change management to strengthen. We look on the tool's side, since the tool is what isn't taking.

The shift

Everyone spoke the same language. Nobody was talking about the same thing.

I start by listening, at every level. A survey of the platform's users, interviews of Program Owners and Project Managers in Chile, Lebanon, Peru and Australia, conversations with the group's marketing management and the program director, then with the Design Labs, Finance and Marketing. Everyone describes a different problem, and everyone is right.

Management

“The platform is mainly responsible for the problems, we need to change that.” What's missing: a macro view of projects by business unit and by program, following progress at a glance, validating, rejecting or cancelling a project on concrete data.

Program Owners and Project Managers

Reporting results is complex and not very intuitive. The platform lacks agility when a project evolves, alternates between too macro and too micro, and no training helps to understand it.

Field teams

An engagement of 2 out of 5 on the platform, and 4 out of 5 outside it. A sense of isolation, and a need for support that emerges quite clearly.

Finance, Design Labs, Marketing

Precise needs and very clear areas of intervention. Finance has no authority over the calculation rules. The Design Labs struggle to spot the projects to step into. Marketing has no concrete link between an offer and a project.

Listening to the interviews again, one word keeps coming back. Project. Then offer. Then initiative. Used sometimes one for the other, never to express a new idea, only to blur the meaning a little. The platform audit goes further: the term “offer” appears nowhere in it, even though the offer is the very substance of a project. Offers become projects, almost by magic, since it is impossible to have a view on anything else.

The sore point lies in “Local Offer”. A local offer can become a “Global Offer” if it meets the conditions in a project. But is it the same project? The rules aren't clear. These are not two things whose definition would simply need fixing: they are two terms that overlap fairly well, and it is the space between them that nothing was holding.

What makes the situation formidable is that nobody is at fault. Everyone decides, prioritizes and reports from their own definition, believing in good faith that they share the others'. No position in the organization lets you perceive the gap: you would have to be in two places at once.

Finding

Six levels, from the executive committee to the teams. Each assigns to the one below, reports to the one above. Nobody is wrong: each only sees their own floor and their immediate neighbor, and reasons correctly from a fragment.

Consequence

Information goes down these six levels one by one and is rephrased at each passage. Six floors down, it is no longer the same thing, without anyone having decided anything.

A poorly shared word doesn't cost a misunderstanding. It costs budgets, trade-offs and months of work in the wrong direction.

The misalignment was therefore not in the platform. It was upstream, in a language believed to be common that wasn't. No interface can fix that. A tool that equips a misunderstanding only industrializes it at group scale.

What was circulating

  • A common language that wasn't: project, offer and initiative replaced one another.
  • The offer, well defined on the Marketing side, absent from the platform: it only circulated there disguised as a project.
  • A macro view and a micro view that met nowhere, from management to the teams.

The instrument

Fix what each term designates, and set out how they connect

The glossary is not a deliverable. It is the instrument that makes the disagreement formulable, a space where everyone's rationalities can finally meet, instead of coexisting without ever crossing.

Its purpose is not to produce official definitions. It is to make it appear, in the same room, that two people who had understood each other perfectly for months weren't talking about the same thing.

Three terms concentrated most of the disorder: project, offer, initiative. Nobody could say how they connected, and everyone took a different connection to be obvious.

Rather than an exhaustive glossary, an offer Pareto: go for the most effective, fixing first the three words that carried most of the confusion.

To settle it, a simple rule: the definition of a term belongs to the entity that stands guarantor for it. The offer falls under Marketing, the project under the Program Owners and Project Managers. As for “initiative”, a catch-all word that covered both others without adding any nuance, it is what kept the confusion going.

Offer
A defined product or service, designed or at least validated by Marketing. An autonomous entity, with its own life cycle, injected into the projects that carry it.
Project
A set of steps that makes an offer concrete at a specific geographic scale, attached to a specific business unit and customer. It has its own life cycle.
Initiative
A catch-all word, used sometimes for an offer, sometimes for a project, with no nuance.

The gain isn't having sorted three words. It is having decoupled two life cycles that were confused: an offer is neither born nor dies with the project that carries it, and a project can host several. As long as the two were held to be one and the same thing, no common steering was possible.

The tool matters less than what it lets us say and do.

From there follows a second, more structuring shift. If the offer has a life of its own, it, and not the project, must serve as the steering unit: the only one that makes sense at every level at once, because it connects the field to the market.

The open field

Once the steering unit changes, everything becomes conceivable

This shift is not a vocabulary adjustment: the offer becomes the backbone of steering. As soon as it is in place, five workstreams open at the same time, when none was formulable as long as everyone steered from their own definition.

“Shift from ‘Project’ steering to ‘Offer’ steering: global harmonization of campaign governance and offer portfolio management to avoid regional fragmentation.”

Nexans' wording.

Five workstreams this shift makes possible

01

A focus on the offer

A template per global offer to ease its duplication across business units, a tag system for granularity, and local initiatives that lean on the global offer rather than bypass it.

02

Better project tracking

A clear view of the stages, from market sizing to scale-up. Simplified tasks, alerts on risks and forecasts, notifications that trigger action.

03

Centralized information

Documents gathered in the M365 environment, accessible from the platform. Documentation learned through use. And an explicit distinction between static and dynamic information, to make steering agile.

04

Accountability and security

Roles defined at each stage of the project and the offer, a sign-off to approve milestones, and calculation rules harmonized from the start.

05

Onboarding and usage

Tool training, program owners and project managers turned into ambassadors with the teams, and shared materials to harmonize presentations.

None of these workstreams was reachable as long as we looked for the problem in the interface. All of them become so together, because they come from the same shift.

The trajectory

A backbone, and a trajectory beyond v1

The offer model doesn't just settle v1: it gives the product depth. From this backbone, a roadmap takes shape: the expected behavior of the teams and change management, consolidated reporting and tracking, cascading the model to other types of programs, continuous adaptation to emerging usage, enriching the vision, and extending the process to other business lines of the group: sales, industry, supply chain.

The roadmap: what the backbone makes possible, well beyond the first batch.

On the design side, this translated into an impact matrix to arbitrate, a framing of the delivery phases, the v1 user stories, and a roadmap that gives product depth beyond the first batch.

You don't design a steering tool. You design what an organization agrees to agree on.

Results

What the redesign produced

Adoption plays out at the bottom of the chain. Project Managers and Program Owners, the Shift Leaders, are the ones who keep the platform alive day to day: this is where the effect is measured.

Before, some usage lived elsewhere: Excel files, third-party tools, what is called shadow IT. Usage now concentrates inside, and shadow IT loses its reason to exist.

  6. Audit of the offer creation processCo-designed with the Product Owner. Recurring semantic misunderstandings on how project and offer connect, and plural participation of actors.
  7. Management interviewTheir view: the platform is mainly responsible for the problems. Their needs: a macro view, following progress at a glance, validating or cancelling on concrete data.

2 · Map

  1. Reflection pointA lack of global vision, a break between the EXCOM and the base, knowledge scattered outside the platform, vague terms despite their importance: project, offer, initiative.
  2. Mapping the organizationCo-designed with the Product Owner. Interactions happen with direct levels, and two zones appear: decision and arbitration, action.
  3. First reflection on vocabularyProject, offer and initiative are used in turn, without ever expressing a new idea.
  4. Second-round interviewsDesign Labs, Finance and Marketing. Each department has precise needs, and they are the ones who hold the knowledge to make the platform evolve.
  5. Platform auditFeatures were added one after another, the database is fragile, static and dynamic information aren't distinguished, and the term “offer” appears nowhere.
  6. Augmented mappingThe new departments step in at many points of the projects. They are the place of adhesion between the head and the legs of the program.

3 · Name

  1. Final reflections on vocabularyDefinitions are the main ground of dissonance. Rule adopted: the definition of a term belongs to the entity that stands guarantor for it.
  2. Completing the big pictureMany improvements depend on features or on information architecture work. The most important problem is the absence of offers on the platform.
  3. A new paradigmAn offer encapsulated in all projects: more ease of access, harmonized rules between offer and project, a process template, better tracking.
  4. A good hook to convinceValorize and harmonize: the project becomes the vehicle for valorizing the offer. A punchy sentence to onboard decision-makers.

My practice?

What this project anchored in my practice

It is often hard to see a system as a whole. You then have to build the place from which to look at it together.

You don't ask people to understand each other better:
you build the place where it becomes possible.

Nobody in this organization was reasoning badly. Everyone held a sound line of reasoning from what they could see from their place, and that is precisely what made the disagreement impossible to find. A silo doesn't prevent good thinking; it prevents knowing what you rely on to think, and therefore suspecting that your neighbor relies on something else.

That is why I didn't start with the interface. A tool doesn't fix a misunderstanding, it industrializes it at group scale. We first had to build a place - a workshop, a common vocabulary, a shared articulation - where what everyone took to be obvious could finally be put on the table and confronted. Before designing what a collective uses, understand what it looks from.

What fed this perspective

Thinking, Fast and SlowDaniel Kahneman

This idea that everyone at Nexans reasoned correctly and was still wrong, I owe in part to Daniel Kahneman and to Thinking, Fast and Slow. It is the book that taught me to distrust the obvious, especially when it is shared.

Kahneman distinguishes two ways of thinking: a fast, intuitive System 1, which concludes before we think, and a slow, costly System 2, summoned only with effort. Everyone in the organization read the word “project” or “offer” with their System 1: an immediate understanding, held to be shared, never put to the test. The disagreement was invisible because nobody had a reason to slow down.

Where Kahneman describes these mechanisms in the individual, I find them between people: the glossary did just one thing, force the switch to System 2 → slow down, make explicit, and discover in the same room that two people who had understood each other for months weren't talking about the same thing.
