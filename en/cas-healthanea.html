The detail

The problem as posed

A fragmented, illegible pathway, hard to follow

Healthanea was an ambitious AXA Group Operations initiative: rethinking how patients navigate complex care pathways. I joined the team as Lead Product Designer, at the crossroads of experience, product and strategy.

My mission as it was entrusted to me: bring clarity to a fragmented landscape, align the team around real needs, and surface opportunities by connecting business, technical and patient concerns.

Clarify the pathway. Streamline the service.
That is what I was asked to look at.

And a starting constraint that seemed prohibitive: no direct access to patients. No way to run my own interviews, no way to observe their difficulties first-hand. The kind of constraint that usually pushes people to fill the gaps with assumptions.

The shift

They weren't lacking a service. They were lacking people around them.

First step, build a research repository from scattered data. I gather and cross-reference everything that already exists: patient interviews about the reality of their experience (chronic patients living with type 2 diabetes), how care systems work, the literature on chronic diseases. The absence of interviews run by me stops being a handicap, it forces me to build a solid overall picture from voices already collected, rather than validating a starting intuition.

Composite example: a schematic, deliberately truncated representation. From the edge toward the center, information is refined. You read sources, draw cards from them, sort them into groupings (the entries of the grid), up to the insight. Around the circle, the directions are the families of sources the information comes from.

What the research brings to light is not a flaw in the pathway. Patients with chronic diseases don't only face medical difficulties: they face loneliness and isolation. And these states are not side effects of the illness, they are directly linked to their ability to stick to a treatment.

Living with a chronic disease means managing physical symptoms, but also the mental load of permanent management, with no certainty of getting better or even of stabilizing. Patients feel alone on this journey, and it is this feeling that makes consistency so hard.

In the silence of loneliness and the weight of isolation, the chronic patient's real need is not the treatment, it is the human bond that gives the treatment meaning.

This finding turns the brief around. The problem wasn't a lack of services: it was a relational and social void that left patients alone in their experience. It was no longer about improving access to care, but about creating the conditions of an environment where the patient feels connected, supported, able to act on their own health, and on that of others.

The brief asked to better connect services. Reality asked to connect people.

What was circulating

Around a chronic patient, the support that was missing.

The instrument

A map to see, not to simplify

Understanding the network of services, actors and needs was not enough. I needed an instrument able to hold the full breadth of the patient experience, something that connects the dots instead of treating each problem in isolation.

The ecosystem map plays that role. It is neither there to look good nor to reduce the system to three blocks: it makes visible what circulates, money, goods, data, and above all where the current flows, and where it breaks. By mapping all the actors and their interactions, it gives the client a clear view of how the system really works: the structuring connections, the misalignments, the opportunities.

Three distinct flows, all the actors, and stars where something can be born.

Healthcare solutions most often concentrate on silos: one isolated aspect of the journey, treated without regard for the whole. But patients don't live in these silos, they cross a web of services, from the doctor to connected devices to the app, most often disjointed. The map doesn't simplify that web: it lets us design with it.

The tool matters less than what it lets us see.

The open field

It isn't a solution that emerges. It's a field.

The moment the map is finished, a veil lifts. The complexity that seemed insurmountable becomes a playground. Ideas pour in, not before but precisely at this moment!

In workshops, we notice with the team members connections that had been there from the start, hidden in plain sight, and opportunities hidden by the apparent disorder of the system.

01

The laboratory / doctor relationship

By establishing a more dynamic and transparent dialogue between these two actors, treatments can finally be personalized and aligned with the patient's real needs.

02

The network effect as a therapeutic lever

A well-designed community gains value as it grows. Here, that value is not social: it is medical.

03

Well-being specialists

Coaches, dietitians, therapists: absent from the existing system, even though they are essential to ensure continuity of care.

04

The doctor as coordinator

Giving doctors and specialists more responsibility, repositioning them as active pilots of the pathway rather than passing prescribers.

None of these directions was reachable as long as we were looking at the wrong problem. All of them become so together, because they come from the same shift. What looked like a web of isolated problems became a network of connected points, with the means to create relationships between them.

Alignment

Aligning strategy, design and execution

Designing within complexity requires alignment. With several stakeholders with scattered expertise and entangled user needs, nobody can work in a silo. The map gave everyone - strategy, design, execution - a shared view and a common goal.

This alignment didn't just avoid gaps: it made prioritization possible. Rather than rushing into designing a product, we took the time to address systemic problems upstream, so that the solution would find its place in the healthcare ecosystem instead of being added to it.

The project was not to “create a product”. It was to design the services that will fit into the existing system, newly revealed, and it is this shift that made it viable over time, rather than merely useful on paper.

In practice, this shifted the conversation with the client: we stopped discussing the app's features to discuss the relationships it could establish between actors who, until then, didn't talk to each other.

The app is only the catalyst of the relationships that form within it, but also beyond it.

What comes next?

A personal extension, not a result

What follows was not built during the mission. It is a personal extension: I asked myself what the map would make it possible to build. It shows what could have been, not what was, and it is not a result.

The case stops before any construction, on a map and a field of directions. What follows starts from there: turning these directions into a product that works.

What circulates outside the market

The original map traces money, goods and data. But isolation, the need the research brought to light, plays out in other flows. Something circulates if it can be given, received, returned or blocked. I added five families: what is given (support, attention, recognition), what is passed on (experiential knowledge), what is delegated (decision, coordination), what commits (a promise made to those around you) and what weighs (mental load, the guilt of forgetting).

These five families are hypotheses. They come from the repository, scattered data and patient interviews already collected, not from interviews I ran myself.

Design principles

01

Start from the leverage points

Not from features: we first look for where a flow breaks, then we build there.

02

Keep the map visible

The patient's ecosystem stays on display, instead of being reduced to a task list.

03

Name the contributors

Each goal says who contributes to it: a loved one, a caregiver, the community.

04

Design for reciprocity, not capture

The patient receives and can give back, with no points or badges that would turn mutual help into currency.

05

An engine and a compass

Each goal has both, so that one keeps the other from drifting.

06

The doctor coordinates, doesn't monitor

Their view is there to spot breaks in flow, not to check adherence.

My practice?

What this project anchored in my practice

A habit can't be created within a simplified pathway inside a product;
it is created through everything you connect around the patient.

I didn't make the pathway simpler. I looked at what surrounded it: the doctors, the laboratories, the community, the professionals and the connected devices, everything that gravitates around a patient and that, until then, didn't talk. Because what makes a treatment last is almost never found in the treatment itself.

This project confirmed something I couldn't yet name at the time: you don't build a lasting behavior by polishing an interface, but by building the conditions that make it possible. Flattening reality produces clean, unanchored solutions; holding it whole produces solutions that last.

What fed this perspective

HookedNir Eyal, with Ryan Hoover

This stance of looking around the product rather than inside it has a source I gladly own: Hooked, by Nir Eyal. It is the book that gave me a first grammar of habit, and the urge to see what lay behind it.

Eyal breaks down the mechanism of products that hook: a trigger, an action, a variable reward, an investment that closes the loop. I kept his rigor, but I moved it: in my work, that loop doesn't play out only on the screen, it is also woven into everything you connect around the person.

Like Ryan Hoover with Product Hunt, it is by embodying the community in the real world, through physical meetings, that the product takes on its full value and creates lasting attachment.
