Nothing stands on its own. What holds, circulates.

We think we live among isolated things. A body, a friendship, a company: each seems to exist for itself, like a unit closed in on itself. Yet nothing stands alone. Everything holds because something passes through it, a word, an attention, a trust, an obligation, a gesture.
Design has long designed things. It has less often designed what circulates between them.

8 min read

Take anything that seems stable to you. A team that works. An institution. A long-standing relationship. An ecosystem. Look at it long enough and the stability dissolves. What you took for an isolated thing, a closed unit, is in reality a flow.

The team holds because information, recognition and trust circulate in it permanently; cut those flows and, within a few months, all that remains is an org chart and people who no longer talk to each other.

It is the most banal observation there is, and yet design long treated it as a backdrop. Myself included. For years, I designed interfaces, services, systems; I made information, users, requests, content and decisions circulate. I was already working in these passages, without necessarily looking at them as the very object of design. I had to go and read elsewhere, in disciplines that weren't mine, to understand what my work had long been bringing to light: that an interface, a service or an organization does not hold first by what it contains, but by what circulates in it.

This text, and the five that follow it, start from this self-evidence, not as a discovery to be proud of, but as a starting point:

What holds is not the thing alone. It is what circulates through it.

A living relationship is not an acquired state: it is a flow rate. The living is recognized by what enters it, leaves it and transforms within it. What no longer circulates is dead, whether it is an organism, a cooperation or a bond between two people. To design, from then on, is not to make better objects. It is to take care of what passes.

To understand why this self-evidence was invisible to design for so long, we have to retrace how it has thought about the human. This is not a trial, it is a genealogy. It doesn't tell of a succession of eras that cancel each other out, but of ways of thinking about the human that have layered on top of each other and whose traces we still carry. Each paradigm grasped something real. And each time, it grasped it as an isolated thing, letting slip what circulated in between.

The long march toward the human, and what it missed

Taylorism · Bauhaus · first half of the 20th century
The human as operator
We design for the efficiency of the gesture: ergonomics, function, utility. The human is a body that produces. This paradigm gave admirably thought-out objects, and beings reduced to their movements. What circulated between operators, tacit coordination, workshop know-how, was not an object of design: it was friction to be removed.
Industrial design · 1950–1980
The human as consumer
We design for desire: style, brand identity, obsolescence. The human has emotions to trigger and a wallet to open. A magnificent visual culture, a deeply asymmetrical relationship to the object, one-way, from product to buyer, where nothing is supposed to come back except payment.
UX · user-centered design · 1990–2010
The human as user
We design for frictionless experience: personas, journeys, touchpoints. This is where “user” replaces “person”. The human becomes a sum of observable behaviors and needs to satisfy. We model, we test, we optimize the funnel. The user is at the center of the journey, but remains the starting unit. We meet them through their behaviors, their needs and their verbatims; more rarely at the place where they are caught in a relationship with others. What two people give each other, owe each other, keep silent about or acknowledge almost never becomes the very object of design.
Systemic design · service design · since 2010
The human as node of a system
We finally design for interdependence: ecosystems, stakeholders, side effects. It is richer, more honest. But the system remains the subject. Relationships become interdependencies to map; yet an interdependence is not yet a lived-in relationship. The map says who is connected, sometimes what passes; it doesn't yet say what that passage commits for those who carry it, what it costs them, or under what conditions it can last.
What these texts propose
The human as being who makes things circulate
We design for what passes between people, not for the isolated person, not for the abstract system, but for circulation itself: what comes in, what comes back, what transforms in the bond. It is a paradigm whose unit is not a thing, but a movement.

None of these paradigms was false. Each grasped a real part of the answer: the gesture, the desire, the need, the node. But each, alone, tended to make that part the whole, and left aside what connected these things to each other.
What these paradigms had in common was not blindness, but a reduction. It has a name, and it runs through the whole century: utilitarianism, in the sense in which the M.A.U.S.S. fights it, that is, the reduction of human relations to interest, equivalence and calculable value.
Yet what circulates in a bond - value of every kind, recognition, trust, obligation, attention - can neither be reduced to utility nor settled like an account. Utilitarianism did not deny this circulation: it was less able to see it than to translate it. We will come back to this point in the fifth text of the series, because that is where it becomes political.

A relationship is not a flow between two nodes

We have to be precise, because one might think systemic design already says all this. It comes close, but stops at the threshold. Mapping flows between actors is not yet working on circulation. An arrow on an actor map indicates that an exchange exists; it says nothing about what is at stake in it.

A relationship is a living space, loaded with implicit understandings, invisible contributions, expectations never voiced, with what each person brings without naming it and what it costs them without their saying so. That is where cooperations are born, hold, or come undone. And they almost never come undone for lack of means or goodwill.

Cooperations rarely fail on the problem posed. They fail because nobody took care of what circulated, or had stopped circulating, between people.

This is a field observation, repeated until it became a rule. Two organizations that share the same values, the same goals, the same resources, and that fail to cooperate. Because someone didn't dare say what it cost them. Because an essential contribution remained invisible and unrecognized. Because a commitment had been understood differently on each side, and the gap never circulated far enough to become sayable. The system was well designed. The circulation was not, nobody had ever taken it as an object.

Even innovation obeys this law

One might think all this holds for the human bond and stops at the gates of the measurable economy, the one that produces and invents. It's the opposite. Where the birth of new things has been studied most, we find exactly the same law of circulation.

Roberto Verganti, looking for where innovation that matters comes from, shows that it comes neither from the market nor from technology alone, but from a change of meaning, and that this change is obtained not by scrutinizing the user under a magnifying glass, but by inserting oneself into a network of interpreters where visions confront each other and circulate. Radical innovation is not a property of an object: it is what emerges when meaning circulates in a fabric of relationships.

David Epstein, looking for where discoveries come from, ends up at the same place by another path: it is not the hyper-specialists who make them most often, but those who make knowledge circulate between distant fields. What passes from one field to another produces what no field produces alone. Discovery is an effect of circulation.

Two inquiries into invention, conducted far from any anthropology, and one conclusion: what is fertile is not the thing, it is the passage between things. It is not a pretty relational idea. It is what we observe everywhere we look at how the new comes about.

And what if the relationship began within oneself

One last consequence remains, more inward, and it overturns a common intuition. We readily believe that taking care of the relationship means taking care of the other: going toward them, understanding their needs, adapting to them. This is what design has done, better and better, for decades.

But circulation doesn't begin in the space between two people. It begins in what each puts at stake in it: what they bring without naming it, what they expect without formulating it, what it costs them and they keep quiet about. Before meeting the other, we arrive with a history, habits of thought, contributions never put into words. Seeing yourself in the bond, before entering it, recognizing what you make circulate without knowing: this is work that design has rarely taken as its object, and that I had to learn otherwise.

Taking care of the relationship means first taking care of yourself: of what you put into circulation without knowing it.

This is not one more tool in the designer's box. It is a change in what we take to be the very object of design. We no longer design the thing; we design the conditions that allow something to circulate, and to keep circulating without betraying itself.

But stating this immediately opens a difficulty. If everything holds through circulation, you still have to see it, and circulation, by nature, cannot be seen from a single place. That is the subject of the next text.

The sources drawn on in this text, and the precise point on which each is called upon.

Innovation & design

  • Roberto Verganti, Design-Driven Innovation: Changing the Rules of Competition by Radically Innovating What Things Mean, Boston, Harvard Business Press, 2009. Drawn on for the thesis of innovation of meaning: radical innovation comes neither from the market nor from technology, but from a change in meaning that emerges from a network of “interpreters”, a circulation of visions, not from listening to the user.
  • David Epstein, Range: Why Generalists Triumph in a Specialized World, New York, Riverhead Books, 2019. Drawn on for discovery as an effect of circulation: it is the profiles able to carry knowledge between distant domains that produce the most advances, not the hyper-specialists.

Genealogy of design paradigms

  • Framework built by the author, the succession operator (Taylorism, Bauhaus) / consumer (industrial design) / user (UX, user-centered design) / systemic node (service design), and its rereading in terms of what each paradigm missed: the circulation between the terms it isolated. The critique of the blind spot of systemic design, people treated as nodes in a network, relationships mapped but never inhabited, extends and shifts this framework.

The anthropological foundations of this thesis, the gift as circulation (Mauss, Caillé), the embeddedness of the economy (Polanyi), are developed in texts 4 and 5 of the series, where they are referenced in detail.
