What makes things circulate. Circulation always has a medium.

The whole series showed that what holds, circulates. One question remained that it never asked: through what? A circulation is never bare - it passes through a medium, a form, a tool. Designing these media is my craft. Here is what theory becomes when it meets the field.

9 min read

Recap of previous episodes

It all started from a reversal: nothing stands alone, and what we take for stable things - a team, an institution, a bond - is alive only as long as something circulates in it. But this circulation is never seen in full, each person perceives only the fragment that passes through them, and a particular threshold is needed for the collective to see it together: metaliminality. What remains is the impulse to make things circulate: it is the gift, with its never quite settled debt, that is its oldest engine. For when nothing circulates anymore, the bond doesn't tighten: it comes undone. This text can be read on its own, it is also the place where everything that precedes becomes concrete and operable.

The missing word

There is a word missing from everything that precedes, and its absence is not an oversight: it is the question the series kept for the end. We said that value, attention and recognition circulate between people. We never said through what. As if circulation floated, disembodied, from one being to another.

Yet nothing circulates without a medium. Recognition passes through a word, a look, a message, a written trace. A collective decision passes through a meeting, a document, a vote, a channel. Knowledge passes through a conversation, a book, a database. Remove the medium, and there is nothing left to circulate: circulation is its medium as much as its content.

There is no relationship without media. What connects beings is always carried by a material form - and that form decides what can pass.

This is where my craft comes in. For these media - an app, a convention, a ritual, a map, an organization - are not neutral. They don't carry pre-existing relationships the way a pipe carries water. They decide what will be able to circulate, in what form, and what will last. To design them is to design sociality itself. And a good share of these media, today, are digital.

The medium is not neutral

The idea is not new. In 1964, the media theorist Marshall McLuhan summed it up in a formula that has remained famous: “the medium is the message”. He means that the form of a channel matters more than what it carries: television, print, the network are not indifferent containers, they shape, in his words, “the scale and form of human association and action”. The medium doesn't add packaging to the message: it determines what the message can be, and what becomes of the one who receives it.

Applied to what concerns us, this gives a design rule of considerable scope. A tool that counts what everyone brings produces calculation: everyone watches their balance. A tool that makes visible what everyone brings, without quantifying it, produces recognition: everyone knows they are seen. Same stated intention, two media, two opposite socialities. The medium makes the relationship, not just the information.

But McLuhan has a blind spot, which the French mediologist Régis Debray pointed out precisely: he is too deterministic. Reading him, technology decides everything, and humans follow. Debray corrects this with a distinction that will become, for my practice, decisive.

To communicate is not to transmit

Debray, founder of mediology, separates two gestures that are almost always confused: communicating and transmitting.

Communicating

Transporting information through space, at a given moment, to as many recipients as possible. It is a matter of signal, broadcast, reach. Fast, wide, now.

Transmitting

Making something last through time, across people and generations. It is a matter of institution, of the medium that persists, of meaning that holds. Slowly, deep, over time.

The whole dominant digital culture is built on communication: maximizing reach, speed, numbers. The viral, engagement, the notification. And this is where my position lies, the one that sets me apart from most digital design: I don't design to communicate, I design to transmit. So that the bond holds when attention drops, that knowledge stays when the person leaves, that cooperation is passed on to those who arrive.

Most digital tools are designed to get a message as far as possible. I design them to make a relationship last as long as possible.

Debray adds a formula that closes the loop on everything that precedes: “difference is not the opposite of harmony, but its condition”. It is, word for word, what the text on the gift said about antagonism: a living system doesn't erase gaps, it makes them circulate. A good digital medium doesn't smooth differences into a soft consensus: it gives differences a place to be spoken, and from there, to hold together.

Sociality is made in the medium

This is not groundless theory: the sociology of the digital has established it. Antonio Casilli, in Les liaisons numériques, dismantles the received idea that the web would isolate us; he shows on the contrary that it produces a new sociability, with its own rules - and, what should stop us, he rereads these rules through Marcel Mauss: circulation online obeys a logic of gift and counter-gift. We post, we share, we reply; we enter cycles of reciprocal obligation that are neither free nor trivial. The gift that text 4 placed at the foundation of the social bond is already, according to Casilli, at work in our tools.

Dominique Cardon, for his part, shows that platforms don't merely host sociability: their design shapes it. What he calls regimes of visibility - who sees what, who is highlighted, what stays hidden - is not a neutral technical given: it is a design choice that decides the form of possible relationships. Making visible is already arranging the bond.

Designing a tool is never designing a function. It is deciding what, between people, will be able to be seen, come back and last.

Metaliminality, in digital space

One question remains that I asked myself for a long time, and that decides everything: does my central concept, metaliminality, still hold once we leave the workshop room for the screen? Common knowledge - “everyone sees that the others see” - seemed to require co-presence, the circle, bodies in the same room.

The answer is that the digital is not the enemy of metaliminality: it is its most powerful lever, and the most dangerous. For a digital tool is exactly the technology that manufactures or destroys common knowledge at scale.

A shared board where everyone sees what the others see, a trace everyone can consult, a space where the state of the collective is made legible to the whole collective: this is equipped metaliminality: the threshold of seeing together, held by a machine, open permanently, without the need to gather bodies. Conversely, a tool that locks everyone in their partial view - their feed, their dashboard, their private notification - methodically destroys common knowledge: everyone knows, but nobody knows that the others know. The collective dissolves into a sum of informed solitudes.

Designing a digital tool is deciding whether the collective will be able to see itself - or whether it will remain an addition of separate views.

This is why metaliminality is not just a facilitator's posture: it is a design criterion. Faced with any arrangement - an app, a channel, a team ritual - I ask the same question: does it make the collective visible to itself, or does it crumble it? Does it manufacture common knowledge, or only distributed knowledge? It is the direct translation, into a gesture of the craft, of everything the series has built.

Three fields, three natures of arrangement

In practice, this work plays out in three fields - and the medium to design is never only digital. It is just as much organizational and methodological, because a convention or a ritual are media in the same way as an interface.

01

Between an app and its users

There, the medium is most literally digital. The question: does the tool let what users give circulate and come back - attention, contribution, mutual help - or does it capture it without return? A mutual help forum, a contribution space, a value card that circulates: so many media of recognition to design, or to repair.

02

Within a team and its trades

Here the medium is mostly organizational. The question: is there a place where what each trade lives and knows can reach the others? A ritual, a channel, a report format: media of transmission between worlds that, otherwise, don't see each other.

03

Around a journey and its moments

There, the medium is methodological as much as technical. The question: does what a person gives at one step get passed on to the next, or is it lost at each handover? A shared trace, a continuity convention: media that hold a journey together beyond those who run it.

And often, all three at once. The same situation calls for a tool (what makes things circulate), a journey (the path that brings it to the right moments, to the right people) and ways of working together (the roles, rituals and know-how that keep it alive and passed on). Designing one without the other two is laying a pipe with no source and no recipient.

TiBillet is the field on which this theory is tested, and whose concrete forms I will soon show. On the cooperative fields where I work, circulation will no longer be a metaphor: a single card for a whole network of venues, a value that passes from one venue to another and leaves a consultable trace, a logic of commons rather than capture. The digital medium will carry there, literally, what the series describes: value that circulates, comes back, and holds a network together, without betraying itself.

What I design, at bottom

We can now name the craft exactly. I don't design interfaces, nor features, nor “user experiences”. I design the media through which a sociality can circulate and last - whether they are made of code, rules or method.

This is what makes this work operable, and not just thinkable. A manager, a team, a project owner doesn't need to subscribe to an anthropology of the gift to understand the question I ask about their tool or their organization: what circulates here, what arrives nowhere, and does the medium let the collective see itself? The theory is behind. In front, there is an arrangement to design, and a bond that, thanks to it, will hold longer than a message.

This is exactly where the three doors of this work meet: the journey that makes you experience the reversal, the series that grounds it, and the practice that puts it to work. This last text is the bridge, the one where the relationship, become circulation, finally finds the medium that makes it hold in the real world.

Mediology & media theory

  • Marshall McLuhan, Understanding Media: The Extensions of Man, New York, McGraw-Hill, 1964 ; French translation: Pour comprendre les médias, Seuil, 1968. Drawn on for “the medium is the message”: the form of the channel shapes the scale and form of human association - and therefore sociality, not just information.

Heart of the text: the distinction between communicating (carrying through space) and transmitting (making last through time), and the critique of McLuhan's technological determinism. The formula “difference is not the opposite of harmony but its condition” loops back to the gift as an arrangement of antagonism.
