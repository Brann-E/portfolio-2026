What no longer circulates breaks. The relationship enables circulation.

It is time to state the thesis plainly, and to prove it through the darkest place: what happens to a world where nothing circulates anymore. We discover that the deeper stake goes beyond the mere framework of design.

10 min read

Four texts have prepared this one. The first posited that nothing stands alone: what holds, circulates. The second showed that circulation can only be seen from a certain place. The third named this threshold - metaliminality. The fourth went down to the foundation: the gift, and its never-settled debt.

It remains to state what all this was heading toward, without detour.

The relationship enables circulation. And circulation, when it lasts, produces reciprocity.

This is not a formula. It is a thesis, in the strong sense - something that can be true or false, and that commits. It states that what we call “relationship” has no substance separate from what passes through it: a relationship is the name we give to a circulation taking place between beings. Where something passes - a word, care, attention, knowledge - there is a relationship. Where nothing passes anymore, the word “relationship” designates only a memory or a label. Two people who no longer give each other anything are not in a cold relationship: they are no longer in a relationship at all, even if the legal bond or the title remains.

The double reversal

This series has taken you on a path in two stages, and it must be made explicit because it is the heart of the method.

The first reversal consists in showing that things touch. Where we thought we saw separate things - services, actors, functions - there are in reality bonds. This is already a precious shift, and a lot of relational work stops there: revealing that people are connected, that nothing is isolated, that the fabric exists.

But there is a second reversal, a deeper one, and it is what makes all the difference. These bonds are not taut, static threads that we would merely have to note. They are flows. A bond exists only as long as something circulates in it; it has no being outside this passage. Showing that things touch is therefore not enough: we must show that it has to circulate, stay alive, on pain of being nothing anymore. The relationship is not a state to preserve. It is a movement to maintain.

We first think the work consists in connecting what was separate. We then understand that it consists in making circulate what, otherwise, freezes and dies.

That is why the true object of this craft is not the relationship - too static, too easy to imagine as something acquired - but circulation. The relationship is what we show first, because it is what people recognize. Circulation is what really plays out underneath. And reciprocity is what circulation produces when it lasts.

Reciprocity: what a system knows how to make circulate

One last misreading must be undone here, the most tenacious. We imagine reciprocity as a balanced exchange: I give you, you give me back the equivalent, we are even. This is the exact opposite of what is at stake - and the previous text showed it with the debt that is never settled.

Reciprocity, as this work understands it, is neither a symmetry, nor a balance, nor an equality of contributions. It is a property of the whole system:

Reciprocity is the emergent capacity of a system to make value, attention and recognition circulate.

“Emergent” is the decisive word. Reciprocity is not in individuals, nor in exchanges taken one by one: it is a quality that is born from the system when, within it, things turn. It cannot be decreed, it cannot be imposed; it emerges - or not - depending on whether the system lets things circulate or blocks them.

This has a consequence that intuition first refuses, and that is yet at the heart of all this work. A deeply asymmetrical system can be highly reciprocal: all that is needed is that something circulates in it and comes back, in another form, at another time. The gift already proved it - the counter-gift never equals the gift, and it is precisely this maintained imbalance that keeps the bond alive. Conversely, a perfectly egalitarian system can be totally sterile: if everyone keeps what they have and nothing passes, equality changes nothing, there is no reciprocity at all.

The right question is never “who gives the most?”. It is: what circulates here, and where is it not happening?

The proof by the extreme: when nothing circulates anymore

A thesis is judged by what it illuminates in limit cases. So let us take the most limit case there is: what becomes of a world where circulation stops altogether? The answer moves this work out of design alone, toward a far more serious stake.

An intermediary is needed first. The economist Karl Polanyi, in The Great Transformation (1944), shows that the economy was long embedded in the social: it was only one aspect of collective life, subordinated to reciprocity, to redistribution, to bonds. Human societies, he says, have known three great forms of economic integration - reciprocity, redistribution, market exchange - and for almost all of history, the market remained framed by the other two. The modern rupture, the one he calls the great transformation, is the moment when the self-regulating market disembeds: it ceases to be a means at the service of bonds and becomes an abstract, impersonal mechanism to which bonds must henceforth submit. The economy is no longer in society; it is society that is summoned to enter the economy.

The disembedding of the market doesn't mechanically produce totalitarianism. It first produces a condition: a world where beings become interchangeable, where a person's value gradually merges with what they produce, earn or serve, and where the bonds of reciprocity that held them in the world come undone.

This is where Polanyi and Arendt meet, in the reading of the sociologist Alain Caillé. The former shows what an order handed over to market mechanics takes away from beings: the social mediations that made the economy a part of collective life, and not its entire measure. The latter names the experience that results when it runs its course: desolation, not chosen solitude, but the condition of one who no longer knows themselves connected to anything or anyone.

Totalitarian domination is founded on desolation, on the experience of absolute non-belonging to the world, which is one of the most radical and desperate experiences of man.

Hannah Arendt, quoted and discussed by Alain Caillé

Totalitarianism doesn't inevitably come out of this desolation; it finds its ground there. It offers beings who have become superfluous a substitute belonging: no longer the living relationship, but the mass; no longer reciprocity, but obedience; no longer a common world, but a common enemy. Racism, antisemitism, imperialism, propaganda and state violence are not external explanations to be added to this reading: they are the historical forms by which an unbound society turns the absence of relationship into artificial unity, then into domination.

The point is therefore not that every market leads to the worst. It is that any order that recognizes value in beings only on condition that they be useful makes the world available for this conversion. When there is nothing left to circulate but equivalence, those who are worth nothing in exchange become, socially, superfluous.

The thread of the series then reaches its darkest point. Utilitarianism, which treats the human as operator, consumer or node - never as a being who makes things circulate - doesn't only produce bad design objects. Pushed to its end, it produces desolation. And where desolation spreads, where unbound beings are no longer connected to anything, the hotbeds of the worst open up.

Where nothing circulates anymore, the bond doesn't tighten: it comes undone. And where the bond comes undone on a large scale, it is not only sadness that sets in - it is the breeding ground of totalitarianism.

This is the real stake, the one the word “design” didn't let you suspect. Making things circulate is not a softness, an extra dose of humanity added to systems that could do without it. It is what holds beings in the world. It is, literally, a vital matter.

What this work does not claim

A strong thesis must state its limits, otherwise it becomes a dogma that explains everything and illuminates nothing.

Not everything is a broken circulation. A blockage can come from a real shortage, a material constraint, a balance of power, a physical limit, legitimately conflicting interests. Claiming that behind every problem lies a bond to repair would be naivety, and a way of fleeing real conflicts.

Some relationships don't need to circulate more. A conflict can be fertile. An asymmetry can be just. A separation can be necessary. A community may need boundaries. Someone may have good reasons not to give - and the text on the gift said so: the unconditional must remain conditional, if only to protect itself from abuse.

One must be able to refuse the relationship. Someone who simply wants to pay, get a service and leave - with no bond, no reciprocity, no transformation of self - must be able to. A relational arrangement that cannot be opted out of is suspect. A circulation that is imposed is no longer a circulation: it is a constraint.

Sometimes, interests are truly incompatible. Not poorly understood, not poorly communicated: incompatible. Discovering that no convergence is possible is a legitimate result, and often a valuable one - it opens arbitration, compensation, the limiting of a power, or conflict owned rather than disguised as misunderstanding.

The triptych

It remains to tie the loop, and it ties itself. This whole journey fits in a circle of three terms, each of which makes the next one possible.

The virtuous circle
1. the relationship opens circulation  ·  2. circulation, by lasting, produces reciprocity  ·  3. reciprocity deepens the relationship - and the circle starts again

This circle is not free, and we must say where it starts and what keeps it going, otherwise it is only a pretty round of words. It starts with the relationship, because it is what shows itself first - “things touch”. It is kept going by circulation, the only one of the three terms liable to stop: you don't stop having a relationship, you stop making something circulate in it, and that is when it dies. And it produces reciprocity, which is not an arrival point but what, once born, restarts the circle by deepening the relationship from which everything started.

To design, within this frame, is no longer to make objects. It is to take care of this circle: to bring it into being where it doesn't exist, to unblock it where it has jammed, to protect it where it is threatened. To create the conditions for value, attention and recognition to keep passing between beings - and, in doing so, to keep them connected to the world.

This is a shift of gaze, not a doctrine. A way of seeing, offered to be tested, contradicted, corrected where it deserves it. But if it holds - and five texts will have tried to show it -, then the most modest thing a designer can do, a message that circulates, an ear at the end of a channel, a quarter of an hour where we say what is going on, ceases to be a detail. It is a fragment of what holds the world up.

Nothing stands alone. What holds, circulates. And what circulates, if we take care of it, holds up the rest.

Economy, embeddedness, totalitarianism

  • Karl Polanyi, La Grande Transformation. Aux origines politiques et économiques de notre temps (The Great Transformation, 1944), French translation by Catherine Malamoud & Maurice Angeno, Paris, Gallimard, 1983 (English original: The Great Transformation, New York, Farrar & Rinehart, 1944). Drawn on for the embedding/disembedding of the economy, the three forms of integration (reciprocity, redistribution, market exchange), and the self-regulating market as the matrix of the totalitarian tipping point.

Desolation and the experience of non-belonging to the world of the isolated individual as a condition of possibility of totalitarian domination (drawn on through Caillé's reading of it).
  • Alain Caillé, Anthropologie du don. Le tiers paradigme, Paris, Desclée de Brouwer, 2000 (reissued La Découverte; in French). Source of the Arendt–Polanyi connection: the link between desolation, utilitarianism (the feeling of being superfluous) and the totalitarian surge.
