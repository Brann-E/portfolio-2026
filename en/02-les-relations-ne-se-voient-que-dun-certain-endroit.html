Relationships, and what circulates between them, can only be seen from a certain place.

If everything holds through circulation, you still have to see it. Yet nobody, from their own place alone, sees what circulates. It takes a particular moment for that, and three families of design discovered it, each on their own, without telling each other.

6 min read

The previous text posited that nothing stands alone: what holds, circulates. One problem remains that this thesis doesn't settle, and it is the real practical difficulty of the craft. Circulation is invisible.

You don't see recognition, trust, obligation, attention pass. You only see their effects, often too late - when someone leaves, when a cooperation jams, when a bond breaks.

And there is worse than invisibility. What circulates is never seen in full, because it is only seen from a position. Everyone in a system perceives only the fragment of circulation that passes through them. The developer sees what passes through them, not what the salesperson absorbs; the caregiver sees what a patient gives them, not what the patient has stopped giving elsewhere. Nobody lies. Nobody sees everything.

Seeing circulation requires a moment where situated views meet, and compose a map that nobody held alone.

This moment is not an extra. It is the central gesture of relational work, and it has a precise structure that anthropology described more than a century ago, about something else entirely. Before getting there, a necessary detour: three families of design stumbled on this same moment, each named it in their own way, and none connected it to the other two.

Three disciplines that ignore each other

Today there are, broadly, three major ways of doing design that proposes to transform something deeper than an interface.

Transformative design wants to change people: it seeks to provoke an inner shift, a questioning of frames of thought.

Systemic design wants to change systems: it maps interdependencies and looks for the points where a small action produces a big effect.

Experience design wants to create moments that leave a mark: it composes lived sequences that are remembered.

Three apparently distinct objects: the person, the system, the instant. And yet, at the heart of each of these methods, we find the same gesture: creating a threshold, a space of passage where what was frozen becomes mobile again, where the old state comes undone before the new one forms. Each has rediscovered, in its own way, a very ancient anthropological object: the rite of passage.

Liminality: what anthropology had already named

In 1909, the ethnographer Arnold van Gennep published The Rites of Passage. He shows that all societies mark the great transitions, birth, puberty, marriage, death, with rites that share the same three-stage structure: separation (leaving one's old state), the margin (being in between, neither one nor the other), aggregation (entering the new state). Van Gennep calls the middle phase the liminal phase, from the Latin limen: the threshold.

Half a century later, the anthropologist Victor Turner seizes on this middle phase and makes it much more than an empty passage. In The Ritual Process (1969), he shows that liminality is the space where identities come undone, where ordinary statuses are suspended, where everything becomes possible again, and where a bond of a particular kind is born among those who go through it together, which he calls communitas: an intense, egalitarian human relationship, freed from the usual hierarchies. Liminality is not a hollow between two states. It is the very place where something can transform, where robust bonds can be formed.

The threshold is not what you cross to get somewhere else.
It is where the transformation takes place.

Let us take our three disciplines again with this key. What they name differently are three entries into the same threshold.

These parallels don't say that these disciplines talk about the same thing, nor that they come from the same origin. They show that at different scales, they meet a similar necessity: creating a moment where ordinary frames loosen enough for a transformation to occur.

This is not an identity, still less a lineage. It is a reading hypothesis: three distinct practices seem to stumble on the need for a threshold.

What cooperation adds to the picture

There is however a decisive difference between classical liminality and what concerns us, and it is what will reopen the question of circulation.

With Van Gennep and Turner, liminality is essentially individual: it is one person who crosses, the initiate, the pilgrim, the newlywed, accompanied by their community which, for its part, stays on the shore. The threshold is lived by a subject, and the group holds it.

What is at stake in a cooperation is of another order: it is an entire collective that must cross together, at the same moment, and come out with a shared vision that none of its members had on entering. It is no longer one person passing while the others watch. It is the group that must see, together, what each only saw in fragments.

And this is exactly the problem posed at the start: circulation can only be seen from a certain place. The threshold a cooperation needs is therefore not only a space of transformation, it is a space where situated views are composed, where the fragment each person perceives joins the others to form the map nobody held. A threshold of pooling perceptions.

It is not enough for everyone to cross. The collective must see
together what, until then, existed only in pieces.

This threshold doesn't open by itself. Nobody, from the inside, can both occupy their position and compose the overall view, it is contradictory. Someone must have prepared the moment, built the space where fragments can be set down side by side without it being experienced as a trial. It is an object of design in its own right, and perhaps the most important of all.

This moment, where a collective sees together what circulates through its bonds, and begins, for that very reason, to be able to act on it, doesn't yet have a name in design. Giving it one, and showing why seeing together is not yet enough to be able to, is the subject of the next text.

References

The sources drawn on in this text, and the precise point on which each is called upon.

Anthropology of the threshold

  • Arnold van Gennep, The Rites of Passage, Chicago, University of Chicago Press, 1960 (original French edition: Paris, Émile Nourry, 1909). Source of the three-stage structure, separation, margin (liminal phase), aggregation, and of the notion of threshold (limen) at the heart of every transition.
  • Victor W. Turner, The Ritual Process: Structure and Anti-Structure, Chicago, Aldine, 1969; see also The Forest of Symbols, Cornell University Press, 1967. Drawn on for liminality as an active place of transformation, and for communitas, the egalitarian and intense bond born of the shared crossing of a threshold.

The three design disciplines

  • Jack Mezirow, Transformative Dimensions of Adult Learning, San Francisco, Jossey-Bass, 1991. The “disorienting dilemma” (disorienting dilemma) as the trigger of transformative learning, the equivalent, on the transformative design side, of entering liminality.
  • Donella H. Meadows, Thinking in Systems: A Primer, White River Junction, Chelsea Green, 2008; and Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System, The Sustainability Institute, 1999. The “leverage points”, the places in a system where a modest intervention produces a deep reconfiguration; the thresholds of the system.
  • B. Joseph Pine II & James H. Gilmore, The Experience Economy: Work Is Theatre & Every Business a Stage, Boston, Harvard Business School Press, 1999. The memorable experience as the unit of value of experience design, the contemporary side of Turner's communitas.
